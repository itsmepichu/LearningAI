# Roadmap

## Agreed scope

- A pure React (Vite) app with heavy use of 3D and motion, hosted as a static site on Vercel.
- Real models in the browser, on the CPU, as opt-in downloads of tens of MB.
- **What may use a GPU:** study material, such as diagrams, 3D scenes and interactive figures, may use the reader's GPU, and should stay light enough for laptops with integrated graphics. LLM training and other practical GPU work does not run in this project: it goes in step-by-step Google Colab guides.
- Deeper versions of all 18 chapters, plus new chapters.
- **Four example projects you choose between:**
  - Harbor stays.
  - Online store, Bank and Travel booking are added.
  - No choice is needed up front: everyone starts with Harbor and can switch at any time.
  - The project you pick decides which practical content, datasets, labs and experiments you see.
  - When another project suits a topic better, a short note at the end of the topic says so.
- **Chapter checks on their own page:**
  - Questions move out of the chapter page to a separate check page at the end of each chapter.
  - The page is grouped by section (one per topic).
  - Each section has **10–12 choose-the-answer questions** and **8–10 likely interview questions**.
- **A course-wide Practice page** (`/practice`):
  - Filter by chapter, question kind, level and example project.
  - Draw random sets of questions and review earlier mistakes, for interview preparation.

### How selection works

- **No choice up front.** Readers do not pick a project before starting. Everyone starts with Harbor, and the course reads normally without ever switching.
- **Switch at any time, from:**
  - a compact picker in the top bar
  - the project tabs on each topic's In practice block, which also offer "Make this my default"
  - the end-of-topic note, when another project fits the topic better
  - the project filter on the Practice page
- The home page shows only a small "Example project: Harbor · change" line, with no onboarding step.
- **Your choice is saved in this browser.**
- **What the choice changes:**
  - **In practice** note: the topic applied to your project.
  - **Failure to avoid** note: the most likely mistake in your project.
  - **Try it** steps: what to change in this topic's interactive figure using your project's data, plus a laptop experiment.
  - **Interactive figures that use data** (chunking, BM25, retrieval metrics, memory, saga, cache keys, queueing…) load your project's synthetic dataset.
  - **Chapter lab variant:** the same structure and pass condition, with your project's data and scenario.
  - **Capstone:** "Build *your project*-lite", with milestones in your project's terms.
  - **Interview questions**, where a project framing helps (for example "design the refund flow for the online store").
- **Peeking without switching:** tabs on the In practice block let you read another project's version without changing your default.
- **Deep links:** `?project=store` in the address opens a topic with that project for this visit only, so links and search results can point at a specific project's version.

## Design: chapter checks

- **A separate page per chapter**, at `/<chapter>/check` (for example `/c5/check`), titled "Chapter 5 check".
  - The chapter page no longer contains a quiz. It ends with the lab and then a card linking to the check.
  - Each topic page ends with a link to its section on the check page ("Check this section: 12 questions · 9 interview questions →").
- **Grouped by section:** one block per topic, in topic order.
  - A section index at the top shows each section's score.
  - Each section is collapsible, because a chapter page holds roughly 130–170 questions.
- **Two kinds of question per section:**

  | Kind | Per section | Mix | Format |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Choose the answer | **10–12** (the current 54 chapter questions move to their sections and count towards this) | Concept, calculation or estimate, and scenario or debugging questions | Single choice or "choose all that apply"; each explains why the right answer is right and why each distractor is wrong |
  | Interview questions | **8–10** | About 3 fundamentals, 3–4 applied and 2–3 system design | The question; its level; a hidden "What a strong answer covers" checklist; a likely follow-up question |

  Example interview question (14.3, system design):
  > *Continuous batching raised throughput but p99 latency got worse. Why, and what would you change?*
  > A strong answer covers: new requests' prefill competes with ongoing decode steps; chunked prefill or a prefill token budget; prefix caching for shared system prompts; measuring time to first token and time between tokens separately.
  > Likely follow-up: how do you choose the batch-size limit?

- **Project framing:** up to 2 interview questions per section may be framed in an example project. The page shows the ones framed in your selected project; an "All projects" switch shows the rest.
- **Totals for the 18 chapters (135 sections):** about 1,350–1,600 choose-the-answer and 1,100–1,350 interview questions. The new chapters add more.
- **Quality rules:**
  - Every question is traceable to its topic's content or a cited primary source.
  - No near-duplicates within or across sections.
  - Distractors are real misconceptions, not filler.
- **Two modes:**
  - **Practice:** feedback after each answer.
  - **Check:** answer a whole section or chapter, then see the score and review your mistakes.
- **Progress:**
  - Scores are saved per section, and interview questions can be marked "practised".
  - The chapter page shows the check result.
  - The progress page shows check scores next to topics studied.

## Design: Practice page (`/practice`)

The course-wide place to drill questions, linked from the sidebar and the home page.

- **Filters:**
  - chapter or section
  - question kind (choose the answer, interview)
  - level (fundamentals, applied, system design)
  - example project (for project-framed interview questions)
  - status (new, answered wrongly before, practised)
- **Modes:**
  - **Random set:** 10, 20 or 50 questions drawn from the current filters, scored at the end.
  - **Review mistakes:** only questions you answered wrongly before, until you get them right.
  - **Interview drill:** one interview question at a time. Answer out loud or in notes, then reveal the strong-answer checklist and rate yourself (missed, partly, covered).
- **Shared progress:** answers and ratings count on the chapter check pages and the progress page too.
- **Loading:** question files load per chapter, and only for the chapters the filters select. All questions together are expected to be about 2 MB of text, so they are never loaded at once.

- The queue simulation's run button draws a new run; the v3 one reused its seed, so it changed nothing.
- BPE and the BM25 tokenizer keep combining marks, so Hindi and Tamil words stay whole.

### Phase 5 — Real computation in the browser (opt-in downloads) ✅

*Delivered: a shared download gate, real embeddings and vector search, a real tokenizer comparison, a small classifier, Python labs, and a Google Colab guide for GPU work. Nothing downloads until a reader opts in, and no model runs on the reader's GPU.*

- **Download gate** (`src/widgets/kit/download.ts`, `DownloadGate.tsx`): the size before anything starts, a progress bar, cancel, failure with retry, a remembered cached state and a clear "not supported here" state, for a worker that holds a heavy runtime.
- **Transformers.js**, in a worker:
  - a real tokenizer comparison (5.1) of GPT-2, BERT multilingual and XLM-R over each project's messages, using the Travel project's 4 languages (English, Hindi, Tamil, Hinglish): about 22 MB
  - real sentence embeddings with all-MiniLM-L6-v2 and vector search over each project's documents: about 29 MB. 9.3 searches beside BM25, 9.4 measures int8 and binary quantisation against an exact scan, and the RRF (9.5) and retriever comparison (9.7) figures keep their illustration until the reader opts in, with each column saying which it shows
  - a small classifier (3.2) that trains a softmax layer on those embeddings, using each project's labelled text, such as the Bank project's dispute reasons, beside a majority baseline. Travel had no labelled text, so the generator gained `requests.csv`, on its own random stream, leaving the other files unchanged
- **Pyodide** labs, in a worker, about 9 MB: the Appendix A script (which reproduces the appendix's reported losses on the manual's own text) and exercises on 2.1, 2.5 and 3.5, with graded checks over each project's dataset. Graders recompute their answers, and Stop or a 60 second limit ends a runaway program without freezing the page.
- **GPU work goes to Google Colab.** 5.6 has a step-by-step guide, from a new notebook and turning on a GPU to giving it back, with code using the selected project's question and document, as cells to copy or a notebook to download.

Decisions that carry forward:
- **LLM training and practical GPU work go to Google Colab**, by the owner's decision. Study material may still use the GPU to draw (see Agreed scope). A WebGPU model chat was built and dropped in favour of the Colab guide. Testing it had also shown that SmolLM2-360M's 4-bit weights return invalid numbers on an AMD GCN 5 GPU, while Qwen3-0.6B answers correctly.
- Runtimes and models load at run time from pinned CDN URLs inside workers, so they add nothing to `npm install` or the page bundles. Each new widget does add its one line to the registry, which is in the main bundle.
- The stated sizes were measured from the hosts' response headers, and are what crosses the network. A new runtime or model version needs them measured again.
- e2e replaces `Worker` with a stub and fails if the main run reaches a model host or starts a worker, so tests never download anything.
- A test runs every lab's solution on a local Python when NumPy is present, and CI installs it.

Checked by hand, once, with real downloads: embeddings, tokenizers, the classifier and all four labs, in Chromium on Windows. The Colab guide's cells ran on a CPU with a tiny random model, which proves the calls work on a current `transformers`.

The Colab guide is a set of cells to copy and a notebook to download, for readers to run in their own Colab. By the owner's decision, the course does not run it in Colab itself: the guide's timings ("a minute or two") are rough estimates and its expected outputs are what the code prints, not a recorded GPU run.

e2e: **a full pass is recorded for phase 5**, on `development` at a868c3b (5 November 2026): every route, every widget, the stubbed download gates, and the axe check with no serious or critical findings.

### Phase 5b — Move GPU practicals to Google Colab ✅

*Delivered: four Colab guides built from data with shared setup and finish steps (5.6 decoding, 6.7 training a small GPT, 13.3 QLoRA, 14.7 vLLM benchmark), the wording that sent readers to rented GPUs replaced across chapters 6, 13 and 14, the capstone, the guide page and Appendices B and E, and a CPU check that runs every guide's code with tiny stand-ins.*

Everything that involves LLM training or hands-on GPU work points readers to a step-by-step Colab guide, like the one on 5.6, rather than to a rented GPU or a passing mention of a notebook.

- **Make the guide reusable.** `colab-guide` is written for 5.6 only. Move each guide's steps into data, and share the setup steps (new notebook, turning on the GPU, running a cell, giving the GPU back).
- **Write the guides:**
  - training a small model, nanoGPT or similar (6.7)
  - a QLoRA fine-tune (13.3), which the chapter 13 lab's substantive fine-tune also uses
  - the vLLM benchmark (14.7), which the chapter 14 lab also uses, measuring what the simulations in chapter 14 only illustrate
  - the capstone's external-compute extensions
- **Update the wording that sends readers elsewhere:** the chapter 14 introduction, the 14.7 lab, the capstone, and the external-compute definitions in the guide page and Appendix E. Tool cards on 6.7, 13.7 and 14.8 and in Appendix B link to the relevant guide.
- **Check practice notes and lab scenarios** for the same wording.
- **What Colab cannot do:** multi-GPU and cluster material (DeepSpeed, FSDP, torchtitan, Megatron-LM, a full nanochat run) is unlikely to run on one Colab GPU. Keep it as reading, say so, and give a scaled-down single-GPU guide where one teaches the idea.
- **Fit the free tier:** some labs state 16–24 GB of GPU memory, which may exceed a free GPU. Choose models and settings that fit, and say what a larger GPU would change.
- **Check each guide's code before it ships,** as the 5.6 guide is checked: every cell parses as Python, and the calls run on a CPU with a tiny model. Guides are not run in Colab by the course (owner's decision), so word timings as rough estimates.

What was built, and the decisions that carry forward:
- **One widget, many guides.** `colab-guide` takes `{"guide": "<id>"}`. Each guide is a `GuideDef` module (`decoding.ts`, `train-gpt.ts`, `qlora.ts`, `vllm-bench.ts`) whose steps are wrapped by `withSetup`, so the setup, the GPU check (always step 4) and the finish step are shared. Each guide figure has an id, `colab-<id>`, that labs, tool cards and appendices link to.
- **6.7, train a small GPT:** nanoGPT's character-level Shakespeare recipe at its full GPU size (about 10.7M parameters), cut to 2,000 steps for a free T4, with float16 and no `torch.compile`. nanoGPT is pinned to a commit. The guide is neutral, the same for every project, since pretraining from scratch takes no project data. A short CPU timing run and one experiment follow.
- **13.3, QLoRA:** Qwen3-1.7B in 4-bit NF4 (float16 compute) with rank-16 LoRA on every linear layer, trained on the selected project's labelled text, the same rows as the 3.2 classifier, in a plain PyTorch loop with loss on the label only. It reports the majority and prompt-only baselines, the gain on a stratified held-out split, general answers with the adapter off and on, the train–test gap, and a save-and-reload. It is the chapter 13 lab's substantive fine-tune. The lab scenarios' extraction tasks need data the datasets do not all have, so the guide teaches the mechanics and the evaluation on classification.
- **14.7, vLLM:** vLLM is pinned to 0.19.0, because newer wheels target CUDA 13, which Colab's runtime did not have (CUDA 12.8) when this was written. Qwen3-1.7B in float16, `vllm bench serve` at 1–32 concurrent requests, throughput and inter-token latency on two charts with the 50 ms target from the 14.7 figure, then a prefix-caching test whose shared prompt is the project's documents, topped up with its recent messages to about 1,500 tokens.
- **Capstone:** no fourth guide. Its GPU extensions link to the 14.7, 13.3 and 6.7 guides, with what to swap in for the capstone. nanochat and GRPO runs stay as reading, since they need several GPUs at once.
- **Labels:** `Colab · GPU` marks a guide that runs on one free Colab GPU. `Needs external compute` now means several GPUs or a cluster, which the course treats as reading. The guide page, Appendix B (with a new "Colab GPU" value) and Appendix E define both.
- **CPU check** (`scripts/colab-notebooks.ts`, then `scripts/check_colab_guides.py`): every guide's notebook, for every project, runs cell by cell on a CPU. Decoding and QLoRA use a tiny random Qwen3-shaped model with a locally trained tokenizer and chat template. nanoGPT is cloned at its pinned commit and trained for a few steps at a tiny size. vLLM is replaced by a mock OpenAI-compatible server and a mock `vllm bench serve` that writes the pinned version's result fields. This proves the code runs on current Transformers 5.22, PEFT 0.21 and PyTorch 2.15. It does not prove that vLLM 0.19.0 or bitsandbytes work on Colab's GPU, or the guides' timings and memory figures, which are estimates.
- **Search:** `scripts/refresh_search.py` rebuilds `search.json` from the pages. Only the one-time importer had written it before, so 140 of 143 topics' search text was stale.

e2e: a full pass is recorded for phase 5b, on `development` at 8542dfe (5 November 2026). It downloads every guide's notebook and runs the axe check on the new guide pages, with no serious or critical findings.

### Phase 6 — 3D and scroll-driven walkthroughs

*In progress, in three batches. Batch 1 is done: the shared scene kit (`src/widgets/scene3d/`), the multi-head attention scene on 5.2 and the exploded transformer block on 5.3. Batch 2 is done: the embedding space (9.3), HNSW layers (9.4), ZeRO sharding (6.6) and the paged KV cache (14.3). Batch 3 is the failover globe and the four walkthroughs.*

Batch 1, as built:
- **Scene kit:** a scene is a list of steps, each a set of tensors whose cells have values and 3D positions. One description drives the 3D view, a 2D view of the same matrices and the text, so they cannot disagree. A tensor that is reshaped keeps its cells, which glide to their new places, or jump with reduced motion. The camera turns with a drag, the arrow keys or buttons, and zooms with + and − or buttons; the scroll wheel stays with the page, and on touch only sideways drags turn the scene.
- **5.2, multi-head attention:** X → Q, K, V → split into 3 heads along the third axis → causal weights per head → weighted values → concatenate and W_O. Real arithmetic, in the browser, on random weights, with a button for new ones. The figure says the patterns are arbitrary and points to the hand-designed heads above it.
- **5.3, the exploded block:** eight floors, from the residual stream through RMSNorm, attention, the add, RMSNorm, the SwiGLU hidden layer (three times wider) and the down projection to the second add, with the residual stream drawn up the side. It is a real forward pass of a tiny block. Each step's text gives measured values, such as each row's RMS after normalising, and parameter counts for the tiny block and an 8B-like one.
- **Not yet checked:** the scenes on a laptop with integrated graphics. They were checked in Chromium with software WebGL (SwiftShader) only, so the owner tries them on such a laptop before release.
- e2e: a full pass is recorded for batch 1, at bbd57a2 (5 November 2026), with no serious or critical axe findings.

Batch 2, as built. The kit gained points, bars, graph edges, notes and a data table, and its 2D view draws points as a map from above and shows the table.
- **9.3, embedding space:** the selected project's labelled messages (the 3.2 classifier's rows) as points coloured by label. They are TF-IDF vectors computed in the browser, or, after the reader opts in to the same download as the figure above, all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings. PCA projects them to three axes, and the text says how much variance the axes keep (about 30% for TF-IDF) and how often a point's 5 nearest neighbours, found in the full space, share its label, against chance. A second view joins one chosen message to its neighbours and lists them in a table. This replaces the planned "labelled illustrative set": the word-count vectors are real and need no download, and are labelled as word counts.
- **9.4, HNSW layers:** the 2D figure's index and search, with the two layers stacked. The steps replay the real trace: greedy hops on the top layer, the drop, and bottom-layer expansions (merged into at most six steps), ending with the result, whether it is the true nearest, and the distances computed out of 300.
- **6.6, ZeRO sharding:** GPUs across, the stage as the third axis, memory per GPU as stacked weights, gradients and optimiser state, with the ZeRO paper's formula (the same as the 2D figure) and a line at the GPU's memory. Later stages come towards the viewer, so the taller earlier ones never hide them. The model size, GPU count and memory are adjustable.
- **14.3, paged KV cache:** the 2D figure's block pool, drawn once per layer (layers 1, 2 and 32 stand for all), with and without the shared prompt. The point it makes: one block table serves every layer, so each token costs 4 KiB per layer and 128 KiB in all for an 8B-like model with grouped-query attention.
- **Checks:** unit tests cover the arithmetic (PCA, cosine, neighbour agreement above chance for all four projects, the ZeRO formula against the 2D figure's, KV bytes per token) and that each scene follows its source (the HNSW trace, the pool's blocks, cube for cube). e2e steps through all six scenes, turns them and checks the 2D view. axe found nothing serious in 3D or 2D, in light and dark, at 1280 and 390 px.
