This page walks through Liu et al. (2023), Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts in plain words. Section and table numbers point into the paper.

1. The problem

Models were being sold with ever longer context windows, but little was known about how well they use what is inside one (abstract, section 1). If a model used its context well, moving the one useful passage from the start to the middle should change little. The paper tests that directly.

2. The method, in steps

  1. Build a question-answering test with a known answer passage. The questions come from NaturalQuestions-Open: 2,655 queries whose annotated answer sits in one Wikipedia paragraph (section 2.1).
  2. Add distractors. A retriever (Contriever) fetches the most relevant chunks that do not contain the answer. Totals of 10, 20 and 30 documents were used (sections 2.1 and 2.3).
  3. Move the answer. The same question is asked again with the answer passage placed first, second and so on, up to last (Figure 3). The score is the share of outputs that contain a correct answer.
  4. Try a task with no reading difficulty. In key-value retrieval, the input is a JSON object of random identifiers, and the model must return the value for one key. Inputs had 75, 140 or 300 pairs, and the queried key was moved around (section 3).
  5. Test several models. The list includes GPT-3.5-Turbo with 4K and 16K windows, Claude-1.3 with 8K and 100K, MPT-30B-Instruct and LongChat-13B, all decoded greedily (section 2.2).

3. What the paper reports

Accuracy forms a U shape: it is highest when the answer is first or last and lowest in the middle (Figure 1, section 2.3). For GPT-3.5-Turbo with 20 documents:

Answer position and accuracy, GPT-3.5-Turbo, 20 documents (section 2.3 and the appendix table)
Position of the answer passageAccuracy
1st75.8%

20th (last)63.2%
No documents at all (closed book, Table 1)56.1%
Only the answer passage (oracle, Table 1)88.3%

So with the answer in the middle of 20 documents, the model scored below what it scored with no documents at all (section 2.3). Claude-1.3 was much flatter, at 59.9% first and 60.1% last for 20 documents.

On key-value retrieval, Claude-1.3 was nearly perfect at every length. GPT-3.5-Turbo and MPT-30B-Instruct were weakest when the key was in the middle, most at 140 and 300 pairs (section 3.2, Figure 7).

The authors tried explanations (section 4). A longer window did not help by itself: with 10 documents, the 4K and 16K GPT-3.5-Turbo models scored 76.8% and 76.9% with the answer first. An encoder–decoder model showed almost no position effect inside its training length, with a 1.9-point gap for Flan-UL2, and the U shape returned beyond it. Putting the question before and after the documents fixed key-value retrieval but barely changed the document task. Instruction tuning of MPT-30B narrowed the worst-case gap from nearly 10% to around 4%.

Section 5 asks whether to retrieve more passages. Reading accuracy stopped improving well before retrieval recall did: beyond 20 passages it gained about 1.5% for GPT-3.5-Turbo and about 1% for Claude-1.3.

4. What it does and does not show

  • Shows: for these 2023 models and these two tasks, where a fact sits in the prompt can matter as much as whether it is there.
  • Shows: a bigger window is not the same as better use of it, and adding passages has diminishing returns.
  • Does not show: that every model has the U shape. Claude-1.3 was flat on the document task, and the authors call their study of causes preliminary (section 4).
  • Does not show: how newer models behave. The models are from 2023, decoding was greedy, and GPT-4 was tested on only a 500-example subset because a full run would cost over $6,000 (Appendix D).
  • The paper recommends reranking relevant documents toward the start or truncating the list (section 5). Treat that as the authors' suggestion, not a tested result.
