Intuition

"The service should be reliable" cannot be checked. "99.5% of requests answered successfully within 10 seconds over 28 days" can. That target is a service-level objective, and the 0.5% it allows to fail is the error budget: room to ship changes and survive incidents.

For AI features, availability is not enough. A service can answer every request quickly and still answer badly, so quality, cost and freshness need targets too.

Mechanism

SLI (indicator): a measured quantity, e.g. "fraction of requests completed successfully within 10 s".
SLO (objective): the target for an SLI over a window, e.g. "99.5% over 28 days".
Error budget: 100% − SLO; the amount of failure you can afford (0.5% of requests). Spending it fast triggers alerts and freezes risky releases (17.8).
SLA: a contractual promise to customers, usually looser than the internal SLO.

Example: why quality needs an SLO too

A deployment keeps 99.95% availability while a silent retrieval bug drops groundedness from 96% to 81% for three days. No availability alert fires. A daily sampled-quality SLO with an alert at 92% would have caught it on day one (17.4).

The numbers

What a budget allows over 28 days (40,320 minutes) and 30 million requests.

  • 99.9%: 0.1% of 30 million = 30,000 failed requests, or 0.1% of 40,320 ≈ 40 minutes of full outage.
  • 99.99%: 3,000 requests, or about 4 minutes.
  • A 45-minute provider outage with no fallback uses 45 ÷ 40 ≈ 112% of a 99.9% month.

Burn rate. 2% errors in the last hour against a 0.1% allowance is 2 ÷ 0.1 = 20 times too fast: the whole month’s budget would be gone in 28 days ÷ 20 ≈ 34 hours.

Code

The program turns five SLOs into the failed requests and outage minutes they allow per 28 days, shows how much of each a 45-minute outage uses, and computes a burn rate. Change REQUESTS or the last hour’s errors and run it again.
