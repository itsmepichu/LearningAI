Intuition

A comments box at a restaurant mostly collects complaints: happy diners rarely bother. Thumbs buttons on AI answers work the same way, so their numbers carry feedback bias: the thumbs-down share is not the share of bad answers.

Feedback is still valuable when it is cheap to give, asks one useful follow-up question, is tied to the exact trace, and is combined with signals users give without trying, such as edits and rephrasing (17.4).

Example: measuring a prompt change

A team ships a new prompt and the thumbs-down rate falls from 1.0% to 0.9%. With 20,000 sessions per version, that difference is well within noise. They instead compare edit rates on drafts, collected on every session, and see a clear drop from 34% to 19%.

The numbers

Biased clicks (illustrative). 12% of answers are unhelpful. Unhappy users click thumbs down 8% of the time; happy users click thumbs up 2% of the time.

  • Per 10,000 sessions: 1,200 × 0.08 ≈ 96 downs and 8,800 × 0.02 = 176 ups.
  • Thumbs down is 96 ÷ 272 ≈ 35% of feedback, although only 12% of answers were bad.

Sample size. Seeing a thumbs-down rate fall from 1.0% to 0.8% needs on the order of 35,000 sessions per version, which is why rare clicks make slow experiments (8.7).

Code

The program simulates 50,000 sessions where unhappy users click more often, compares the thumbs-down share with the true unhelpful rate, counts reasons, and estimates the sessions needed to see an improvement. Change the click rates and see how the bias moves.
