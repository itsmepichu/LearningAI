A data mixture is the proportion of each source in training (e.g. 60% web, 15% code, 10% books and papers, 8% multilingual, 7% maths). Mixtures are tuned with small-scale experiments because they strongly shape capabilities. Often a higher-quality slice is upweighted near the end of training ("annealing").

Scaling laws are empirical relationships: loss falls smoothly and predictably as a power law in parameters N, tokens D and compute C. Two results are worth memorising:

  • Training compute ≈ 6 · N · D FLOPs (2 for the forward pass, 4 for backward, per parameter per token).
  • Chinchilla-optimal (2022): for a fixed compute budget, loss is minimised with roughly 20 tokens per parameter. Modern models are deliberately trained far past this (hundreds or thousands of tokens per parameter) because a smaller model trained longer is cheaper to serve, and serving costs dominate over a model's lifetime.
Example: an 8B model on 15T tokens

C = 6 × 8×10⁹ × 15×10¹² = 7.2×10²³ FLOPs. At 40% MFU (≈ 4×10¹⁴ FLOP/s per GPU) that is ≈ 1.8×10⁹ GPU-seconds ≈ 500,000 GPU-hours, the same order of magnitude as published figures for models of this size. At an illustrative $2.50 per GPU-hour, ≈ $1.25M for the final run alone. This is why enterprises adapt existing models rather than pretrain.
