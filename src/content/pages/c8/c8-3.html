Intuition

A model judge is like a new marker joining a team of teachers: before trusting their grades, you have both mark the same pile of papers and compare. You will find habits, such as giving higher marks to longer essays or to whichever essay they read first.

And every score is uncertain. A test of 200 questions is a sample, so the score could move a few points with a different 200. Reporting a range, not just a number, stops teams celebrating noise.

Mechanism

Known judge biases: position bias (prefers the first or second answer in pairwise comparisons), verbosity bias (prefers longer answers), self-preference (prefers outputs from its own model family), style over substance (confident formatting), and leniency on technical errors it cannot detect. Mitigations: swap positions and average, use claim-level rubrics, control length, use a different model family as judge, and give the judge the reference answer when one exists.

Calibrating a judge: have humans label 100–300 cases, run the judge on the same cases, and measure agreement. Use Cohen’s κ (agreement corrected for chance) rather than raw percent agreement, because if 90% of answers are good, a judge that always says "good" agrees 90% of the time while knowing nothing. Also check the judge's precision and recall for catching bad answers, the cases you care about.

Related cases need grouped resampling. When cases are not independent (several turns of one conversation, several questions about one document, repeated runs of one task, or many requests from one tenant), resample whole groups rather than individual rows: a cluster bootstrap. Treating correlated rows as independent makes the interval look narrower than it is.

Evaluation uncertainty comes from a finite test set, from non-deterministic outputs (run each case 3–5 times and report variance), and from judge noise. Report a confidence interval (bootstrap: resample cases with replacement 1,000 times and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the metric). For comparing two systems on the same cases, use paired differences.

Example

A team switches judges and suddenly "helpfulness" rises from 3.9 to 4.3 with no system change. The new judge is more lenient. Lesson: freeze the judge model and prompt as part of the evaluation suite version, and re-calibrate against humans whenever it changes.

The numbers

Kappa from the figure’s default counts. Judge and humans agree on 160 + 15 = 175 of 200 cases: 87.5%.

  • The judge says "good" 178 times (89%) and humans 167 times (83.5%). Two markers guessing at those rates would agree by chance about 0.89 × 0.835 + 0.11 × 0.165 ≈ 76% of the time.
  • The most they could improve on chance is 100 − 76 = 24 points; they achieved 87.5 − 76 = 11.5 of them.
  • Kappa is 11.5 ÷ 24 ≈ 0.48: only moderate agreement, despite the impressive 87.5%.

A bootstrap range. Re-drawing the 200 cases 1,000 times and recording the score each time, then taking the middle 95% of those scores, gives the range to report, such as "84% (79% to 88%)".
