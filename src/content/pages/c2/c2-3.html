Intuition

Imagine standing on a foggy hillside, trying to reach the valley floor. You cannot see the valley, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you take a step downhill, feel again, and take another step. That is gradient descent, and it is how every neural network learns.

The height of the hill is the loss: how wrong the model is. The direction you feel is the gradient. The size of your step is the learning rate: tiny steps take forever, and giant leaps jump right over the valley.

Mechanism

Derivative f′(x): how fast f changes when x changes a little; the slope.
Gradient ∇f: the vector of partial derivatives, one per parameter. It points in the direction of steepest increase.
Loss function: a single number that measures how wrong the model is. Training minimises it.
Gradient descent: repeat θ ← θ − η·∇L(θ). Step against the gradient by a learning rate η.
Chain rule: if y = f(g(x)) then dy/dx = f′(g(x))·g′(x). Backpropagation is the chain rule applied through every layer.

A model with 8 billion parameters has a loss landscape in 8 billion dimensions. You cannot see it, but the gradient still tells each parameter which direction lowers the loss locally. Training is millions of small downhill steps. The learning rate is the most important setting: too small and training crawls; too large and the steps overshoot, the loss oscillates or explodes (often showing up as NaN).

From one parameter to two, in 3D. Real models have millions of parameters, but two are enough to see what an optimiser does. Below, three update rules start from the same point on the same surface. Momentum keeps a running velocity, so it speeds up on long slopes and can roll through small dips. Adam divides each step by a running estimate of the gradient's size, so every parameter moves at a similar pace however steep its direction is. Chapter 4.2 covers how they are used in training.

Example: one manual step

Fit y = w·x to the single point (x=2, y=6). Loss L = (w·2 − 6)². Gradient dL/dw = 2·(2w − 6)·2. At w = 1: gradient = −16. With η = 0.05: w ← 1 − 0.05·(−16) = 1.8. Next step: gradient = 2·(3.6−6)·2 = −9.6, w ← 2.28. It converges toward w = 3.

The numbers

The step size decides everything. In the example above, at w = 1 the slope is −16 (the loss falls as w grows):

The chain rule, in numbers. Suppose a model has three stages in a row. Nudge the input by a little, and the first stage’s output moves 2 times as much; that moves the second stage’s output 3 times as much again, and the third 2 times as much again. The input moves the final output 2 × 3 × 2 = 12 times as much. Backpropagation does that multiplication for every one of a model’s billions of parameters, at every training step.
