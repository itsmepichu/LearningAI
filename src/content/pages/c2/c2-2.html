Intuition

Probability is about counting what usually happens. The trap is forgetting how rare something is to begin with. If one transaction in two hundred is fraud, then even a very good fraud alarm will ring mostly for honest customers, simply because there are so many more of them.

The second trap is trusting small tests. Toss a coin ten times and you might get seven heads; that does not make it unfair. A score measured on a hundred test cases wobbles the same way, so two prompts that score 82% and 86% might be equally good.

Mechanism

Probability distribution: how likely each possible outcome is; the probabilities sum to 1. An LLM's output at each step is a distribution over its vocabulary.
Conditional probability P(A | B): probability of A given that B is true. Language modelling is P(next token | previous tokens).
Bayes' theorem: P(A | B) = P(B | A)·P(A) / P(B). It flips a conditional around and forces you to account for the base rate P(A).
Sampling: drawing outcomes at random according to a distribution. Generation samples tokens; evaluation samples test cases from all possible inputs.
Confidence interval (CI): a range that, under the method's assumptions, would contain the true value in (say) 95% of repeated experiments. It expresses how much your measurement could be off because you only tested a sample.

Two places this bites engineers. First, base rates: a fraud model with 95% recall and a 2% false positive rate sounds excellent, but if only 0.5% of transactions are fraudulent, most flagged transactions are still legitimate. Second, small evaluation sets: "our new prompt scores 86% vs 82%" on 100 test cases is within noise. The 95% CI for 86/100 is roughly 78%–91%.

Example: is the new model better?

Model A passes 412/500 cases (82.4%), model B 431/500 (86.2%). Their 95% intervals are about 78.8–85.5% and 82.9–88.9%, which overlap. Because both models ran on the same cases, a paired test (count cases where only A passed vs only B passed, then a sign/McNemar test) is more powerful and is the right next step (8.3).

The numbers

Bayes by counting. 10,000 transactions, 0.5% of them fraud, and a detector that catches 95% of fraud and wrongly flags 2% of honest transactions:

  • Fraud: 10,000 × 0.5% = 50 transactions. Caught: 50 × 95% ≈ 48.
  • Honest: 9,950 transactions. Wrongly flagged: 9,950 × 2% = 199.
  • Flagged in total: 48 + 199 = 247, and only 48 of them are fraud: 48 ÷ 247 ≈ 19%.

Four out of five alarms are false, even though the detector sounds excellent.

How much a score wobbles. A prompt passes 86 of 100 test cases. The 95% confidence interval is roughly 78% to 91%: the true pass rate could be anywhere in there. With 400 cases and the same 86%, it narrows to about 82% to 89%. Four times the cases halves the width.
