Intuition

Finding the most similar vector by checking every single one is like finding the nearest café by visiting every café in the country. It works for a few thousand; it does not work for ten million at a hundred questions a second.

Approximate search uses a map. HNSW is like taking motorways to the right region, then main roads, then side streets. IVF is like going straight to the few neighbourhoods closest to you and checking only those. You might occasionally miss the very closest café, but you get there in a fraction of the time.

Mechanism

Exact (brute-force) vector search compares the query with every vector: for 10 million 768-dimension vectors that is 7.7 billion multiply-adds per query. Fine for thousands of vectors, too slow for millions at high query rates. Approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) indexes trade a little recall for orders-of-magnitude speed.

  • HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World graphs): each vector is a node linked to its nearest neighbours. Upper layers are sparse "express lanes" with long links; the bottom layer has all nodes. Search starts at the top, greedily moves to the neighbour closest to the query, drops a layer, and repeats. Parameters: M (links per node; memory and recall), efConstruction (build quality), efSearch (candidates explored at query time; recall vs latency).
  • IVF (inverted file): cluster vectors with k-means; at query time search only the nearest nprobe clusters.
  • Quantisation shrinks vectors: scalar (float32 → int8, 4× smaller), product quantisation (PQ) (split vector into sub-vectors, store each as a codebook ID; 16–64× smaller), binary (1 bit per dimension, 32× smaller, used with re-scoring). Common pattern: search compressed vectors, then re-score the top candidates with full-precision vectors.
  • DiskANN-style indexes keep the graph on SSD for collections larger than RAM.
Example: tuning efSearch (illustrative numbers)

On a 5M-vector index, efSearch = 40 gives Recall@10 = 0.91 against exact search at 2 ms; efSearch = 200 gives 0.985 at 7 ms. Measure this recall-vs-latency curve on your own data and queries (10.8); the right point depends on how much a missed chunk costs.

The numbers

The cost of checking everything. 10 million vectors of 768 numbers is 10,000,000 × 768 ≈ 7.7 billion multiplications per question.

Checking a few cells instead. In the program below, 20,000 vectors are grouped into 100 cells. Searching the 3 cells nearest the question compares about 700 vectors, under 4% of them, and still finds about 97% of the true top 10.

Memory. Those 10 million vectors take 10,000,000 × 768 × 4 bytes ≈ 30.7 GB as float32, 7.7 GB as int8, and under 1 GB as 1 bit per number, which is why big indexes are quantised and the top results re-checked with full vectors.

Code
