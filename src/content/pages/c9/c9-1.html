Intuition

RAG is an open-book exam. Instead of hoping the model memorised your company’s policies, you look up the relevant pages for each question and hand them over with the question: "answer from these, and say which page you used". When a policy changes, you swap the page; nothing needs retraining.

Fine-tuning is different: it is practice, not a textbook. It changes how the model behaves, such as its format, tone or decision style, but it is a poor way to teach facts that change every week.

Mechanism

RAG solves a knowledge problem: the model lacks facts (private, recent or long-tail) or you need answers traceable to sources. Fine-tuning solves a behaviour problem: the model knows enough but does not respond in the format, style, vocabulary or decision pattern you need. Fine-tuning is a poor way to inject facts: it is expensive to update, hard to delete from, gives no citations, and models recall fine-tuned facts unreliably.

Example

A bank wants an assistant for its 3,000 internal policies that change weekly. RAG: re-index on change, cite policy IDs, filter by employee role. Later, evaluation shows answers are correct but not in the mandated "Decision / Basis / Exceptions" format, and few-shot prompting costs too many tokens. A small fine-tune on 2,000 formatted examples fixes format; RAG still supplies the facts.

The numbers

Why not send everything? 3,000 policies averaging 1,500 tokens is 3,000 × 1,500 = 4.5 million tokens, more than 20 times a 200,000-token context window. Retrieving 5 passages of 500 tokens sends 2,500 tokens per question: under a thousandth of the collection.

Freshness. A changed policy can be re-indexed in minutes. Getting the same change into a fine-tuned model means new training data, a training run and a new evaluation, usually days or weeks.

Cost at volume (illustrative). At $1 per million input tokens and 100,000 questions a month, 2,500 tokens per question costs about $250 a month; 50,000 tokens per question, from sending far more than needed, would cost $5,000.
