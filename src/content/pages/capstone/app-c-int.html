Intuition

In an interview or a design review, the strongest answer is a story with evidence: the task and its risk, the simplest thing that worked, the failure that justified each extra part, and how the system is operated.

Every number in that story carries its evidence category: simulated, measured locally, projected, or operated in production. Saying "projected, not yet validated" shows judgement; letting a projection pass as a measurement loses trust the moment someone asks.

Mechanism

  1. The task and its risk: who uses it, what a wrong answer costs, and the acceptance criteria you wrote first.
  2. The simplest baseline and its measured numbers.
  3. The failure that justified each extra component (reranker, agent step, fine-tune), with the paired measurement that showed it helped.
  4. Operational limits: how you handle permission revocation, duplicate tool execution, stale indexes, model upgrades, failed rollouts, quality drift and cost per successful task.
  5. The evidence category of every claim: simulated, measured locally, projected, or operated in production (Appendix E). Saying "projected, not yet validated" is a strength, not a weakness.
Example answer fragment

"BM25 alone reached Recall@10 = 0.71 on our 100 labelled questions; hybrid plus a reranker reached 0.88, a paired improvement whose 95% interval excluded zero. Answer groundedness was 96% on a 60-case held-out set. On my laptop the service handled 4 requests/s with a mocked model at p95 1.2 s; that measures orchestration only. For production I projected GPU needs from vLLM benchmarks I have not yet run, so that figure is a projection."

The numbers

The example fragment, checked.

  • Recall@10 from 0.71 to 0.88 on 100 questions: 17 more questions answered, measured locally.
  • 4 requests a second at a p95 of 1.2 seconds with a mocked model: by Little’s law about 4 × 1.2 ≈ 5 requests in flight. It measures orchestration only, because the model was mocked.
  • GPU needs from benchmarks not yet run: projected, and saying so is the right answer.

Code

The program checks a short write-up sentence by sentence: every number needs an evidence category, and words like "never" need to say what was tested. Add a sentence of your own capstone write-up and run it again.
