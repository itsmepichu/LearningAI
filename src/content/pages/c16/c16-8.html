Intuition

Laws and standards for AI (AI regulation and frameworks) mostly ask engineering questions in legal words: what is this system for, who could it harm, how do you know it works, can a person step in, and can you show your evidence?

The engineer’s job is to know which rules apply, with legal and compliance colleagues, turn each into a concrete control, and make the evidence fall out of the normal pipeline: evaluation reports, logs, model cards and approvals.

Mechanism

Requirements depend on jurisdiction, sector, use case and your role (developer, deployer, provider of a general-purpose model). The engineering task is to know which apply, translate them into controls, and produce evidence. This section is orientation, not legal advice; involve legal and compliance early.

Example: two use cases, two tiers

An internal assistant that summarises engineering docs is registered as low risk: standard data controls, a transparency notice, and the normal evaluation gates. A CV-screening assistant for recruiters touches employment decisions, which many frameworks (including the EU AI Act's high-risk categories) treat as high risk: it needs documented risk management, bias evaluation across groups, human review of every recommendation, detailed logging, and legal sign-off before launch.

The numbers

An inventory (illustrative). A company with 25 AI use cases might find 15 low risk, 8 medium and 2 high.

  • Low: 3 standard controls each, mostly automatic.
  • High: about 6 controls each, including human review of every decision. At 400 decisions a day and 3 minutes per review, that is 400 × 3 = 1,200 minutes, 20 hours of reviewer time a day for one use case.

That cost is why the tiering decision is made early, before the system is built.
