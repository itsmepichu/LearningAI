Intuition

A trained model has many weights that barely matter, like the rarely used tools in a big toolbox. Pruning takes them out; quantisation (14.4) stores the rest with fewer bits. Together they make a model smaller and faster, at some cost in quality.

Taking things out always hurts a little, so the usual recipe is to prune, then train briefly again, ideally with the original model as a teacher, so the smaller model recovers what it lost.

Mechanism

TechniqueWhat is removed or shrunkSpeed-up in practice
Unstructured pruningindividual small weightslittle, unless hardware supports sparse maths
Structured pruningwhole heads, neurons or layersreal: the matrices get smaller
2:4 sparsitytwo of every four weights, in a pattern GPUs accelerateup to about 1.5–2× on supported GPUs
Quantisationbits per weight (16 → 8 → 4)large for decoding, which is memory-bound (14.4)
Prune + distillayers or width, then retrain from the originalsmaller models at close to the original quality
Sparse is not always fast

Zeroing half the weights saves nothing if the hardware still multiplies the zeros. Measure speed on your serving stack before counting a saving.

The numbers

Sizes. A 3-billion-parameter model in 16-bit takes 3 × 2 = 6 GB. Structured pruning to 2 billion parameters: 4 GB. Then 4-bit quantisation (about 0.55 bytes per weight): 2 × 0.55 = 1.1 GB, small enough for a phone.

Decode speed (memory-bound, 14.4). Reading 6 GB per token at 80 GB a second allows at most about 13 tokens a second; reading 1.1 GB allows about 73.

The quality cost (illustrative). If the original scores 90% and the pruned model 84% before recovery and 88% after, recovery won back 4 of the 6 points lost.
