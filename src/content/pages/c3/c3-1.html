Intuition

There are three ways to learn, and they differ in the feedback you get. With a teacher who marks every answer, you learn from right answers: that is supervised learning. With a pile of photos and no captions, you can still sort them into groups that look alike: that is unsupervised learning. And like learning to ride a bike, you can try things and learn from how well they went: that is reinforcement learning.

LLMs use a clever fourth trick. They hide the next word of real text and try to guess it, so every sentence on the internet becomes a free lesson with its own answer.

Mechanism

Supervised learning: learn from input–output pairs (labels). "Email → spam/not spam", "house features → price".
Unsupervised learning: find structure in inputs without labels: groups (clustering), compressed representations, anomalies.
Self-supervised learning: create labels from the data itself, e.g. hide the next word and predict it. LLM pretraining is self-supervised, which is why it can use trillions of tokens without human labelling.
Reinforcement learning (RL): an agent takes actions in an environment, receives rewards, and learns a policy that maximises total reward. Used in RLHF and reasoning-model training (6.5).
Example: one company, three paradigms

An e-commerce site trains a supervised model to predict return probability from order data, uses unsupervised clustering to discover customer segments for marketing, and uses RL-style bandit algorithms to choose which recommendation layout to show, rewarded by clicks.

The numbers

Why labels are the bottleneck. 10,000 labelled examples at 30 seconds each is 300,000 seconds: about 83 hours of someone’s time, before any checking.

Why self-supervision scales. Every token in a text is a question with a known answer: "what comes next?". A 1,000-token page gives 1,000 lessons for free. A model trained on 15 trillion tokens gets 15 trillion lessons, with no one labelling anything.

Reinforcement by trial. A learner choosing between three reply styles that succeed 5%, 12% and 8% of the time tries each one, keeps a running score, and picks its best so far 9 times in 10. After a few thousand tries it is choosing the 12% style most of the time, without ever being told which was best.

Code

The program runs a tiny example of each kind on your example project’s data: it predicts a number from a group average learned from labelled rows, groups the same numbers into three clusters with no labels, and lets a learner find the best of three options by trial and reward. Change TRUE_RATES, or the 0.1 that sets how often it explores, and run it again.
