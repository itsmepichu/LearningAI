Intuition

A model can only be as good as the examples it learns from. If the labels are inconsistent, it learns the inconsistency; if the same row appears twice, it may simply memorise it. So most of the work in a real project is cleaning data and writing clear labelling rules, not choosing models.

Rare events need special care. If only 3 transactions in 1,000 are fraud, a lazy model can say "not fraud" every time and be right 99.7% of the time while catching nothing. You have to tell it that missing a fraud matters more.

Mechanism

Data quality usually matters more than model choice. Most of a real ML project is spent here.

  • Cleaning: remove duplicates, fix inconsistent formats ("NY", "New York"), handle missing values (drop, fill, or add a "missing" indicator), cap impossible values (age 210).
  • Labelling: humans (or rules, or models) attach the correct output. Write a labelling guideline with definitions and edge cases, have two people label a sample, and measure inter-annotator agreement (e.g. Cohen's κ). Low agreement means the task is ambiguous, and no model can beat that ceiling.
  • Feature engineering: turn raw data into model inputs: dates into "day of week", text into word counts, amounts into log scale, transactions into "count in last 24h". Deep learning learns features itself; tabular ML still benefits enormously from good features.
  • Class imbalance: when one class is rare (fraud 0.3%), a model can ignore it and still score high accuracy. Remedies: use precision/recall instead of accuracy, class weights (penalise mistakes on the rare class more), resampling (oversample rare or undersample common), and tuning the decision threshold.
Example: a labelling guideline excerpt

Label "urgent" if the customer reports service unavailable, data loss, or a security concern. Do not label urgent for angry tone alone. Edge case: "I can't log in" is urgent only if it affects more than one user or the account owner. Two annotators label 200 tickets; κ = 0.58 reveals disagreement on "can't log in", so the guideline is revised before labelling 10,000 more.

The numbers

Accuracy that means nothing. 10,000 transactions, 30 of them fraud. A model that never says fraud is right 9,970 times: 99.7% accuracy, 0% recall.

Class weights. There are 9,970 ÷ 30 ≈ 332 normal transactions for every fraud. Giving each fraud 332 times the weight in training makes the two classes count equally, so missing one fraud costs the model as much as wrongly flagging 332 normal transactions.

Agreement beyond chance. Two people label 200 tickets and agree on 170: 85%. But with these labels they would agree 70% of the time just by chance. The possible improvement over chance was 30 points (from 70% to 100%), and they achieved 15 of them: a Cohen’s kappa of 15 ÷ 30 = 0.5, which says the guideline needs work.
