Intuition

Risk-based AI laws work like building codes: a garden shed needs almost nothing, a house needs inspections, a hospital needs far more, and some constructions are simply banned. The EU AI Act is the best-known example: it sorts AI uses into tiers and gives each tier its own duties.

Duties also depend on your role. The provider who builds the system and the deployer who uses it carry different obligations, and a company can be both for different systems.

Other regimes. Many countries regulate AI mainly through existing laws: privacy (the GDPR in Europe, India’s Digital Personal Data Protection Act and Rules), consumer protection, and sector rules for banking, health and hiring. A system can be minimal risk under one law and heavily regulated under another.

Example: two tiers in one company

A bank’s internal document summariser is minimal risk; its loan pre-qualification model is high risk, so it needs documented risk management, data governance, logging, human oversight and testing for accuracy and bias before release. Most of these controls already exist in chapters 8, 16 and 18; the work is mapping and evidence.

Orientation for engineers, not legal advice. Laws, guidance and application dates change; confirm what applies with your legal and compliance team.

The numbers

An inventory (illustrative). 30 AI uses: 1 needs redesign because it is close to a prohibited practice, 3 are high risk, 9 have transparency duties, 17 are minimal.

  • If a high-risk system needs about 10 controls with evidence and a transparency-duty system about 2: 3 × 10 + 9 × 2 = 48 rows in the control matrix.
  • The 17 minimal-risk uses still need entries in the inventory, so the classification itself can be checked.
